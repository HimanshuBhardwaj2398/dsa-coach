# Predict, trace, remember: engineering a DSA tutor

Himanshu should build one portable Agent Skill (a stage-gated teaching protocol plus a deterministic pipeline that turns instrumented Python runs into interactive HTML) and keep it, his concept pages and his learner model in a single git "notebook" repo. Claude Code is where the skill is built, verified and curated; the Claude app, through a Cowork project and published artifacts, is where he studies, asks follow-ups and reviews. Three choices matter more than any feature. Every visual must make him act (predict the next pointer move, fill a hidden cell, retrace with the picture hidden), because passive algorithm animation performs about as well as no visualization and motion alone makes material feel easier while hurting transfer ([Naps et al. 2002](https://scispace.com/pdf/exploring-the-role-of-visualization-and-engagement-in-bhelf2687d.pdf); [Myller 2009](https://cs.uef.fi/pub/Dissertations/myller.pdf); [Paik 2010](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/thesesdissertations/678)). Every frame must come from executing the real Python and be drawn by a fixed component library, because splitting execution from rendering lifted LLM-generated visualization success from **82.5% to 99.8%** ([ALGOGEN](https://preview.aclanthology.org/ingest-acl/2026.findings-acl.156/)). And the memory of "what works for him" must credit a technique only when he re-solves a problem unaided days later, kept in files rather than Claude's opaque memory: answer-giving AI raised practice scores 48% while cutting exam scores 17% ([Bastani et al.](https://hamsabastani.github.io/education_llm.pdf)), and user memory profiles raised AI agreement sycophancy by as much as 45% for one model ([Jain et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12517)). The curriculum must train the two modes that 2026 loops test side by side: timed, unaided DSA at roughly LeetCode-medium level (plus Python machine coding for Indian product companies), and plan-first, verify-everything AI-assisted coding. Planning programs in his head is trainable by externalizing state (plan cards, invariant diagrams, complete trace tables) and then fading those scaffolds as his accuracy rises. His preference for visual, step-by-step material is a sound default format, but the evidence credits engagement and segmenting rather than style matching, so the skill should treat "visual works for me" as a hypothesis to test against delayed re-solves.

## Interview loops now test unaided recall and AI judgment side by side

The first design question is what to train, and the answer is narrower than "grind LeetCode" and broader than "AI engineering". Algorithmic coding still gates every segment Himanshu targets. Among 1,765 AI-engineer job descriptions, the roughly 80 that described a process had a **median of four steps**, and candidate reports keep describing 45–60-minute LeetCode-style rounds, "sometimes AI-flavored" ([AI Shipping Labs](https://aishippinglabs.com/blog/ai-engineering-interview-process); [AI Engineering Field Guide](https://github.com/alexeygrigorev/ai-engineering-field-guide/blob/main/interview/01-interview-process.md)). In a September 2025 survey, **none of 53 Big Tech interviewers said their company had dropped algorithmic questions**, though 58% had changed their questions because of AI ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pulse-146)). The level sits near LeetCode medium. Meta MLE onsites reportedly ask two mediums in 45 minutes, while Google, Databricks and similar loops ask one hard problem with follow-ups ([Yuan Meng](https://www.yuan-meng.com/posts/mle_interviews_2.0/)); IBM's AI-engineer live round used three easy-to-medium questions, the level Grigorev suggests targeting in AI-engineer prep ([AI Shipping Labs](https://aishippinglabs.com/blog/ai-engineering-interview-process)). What differs by segment is the coding wrapped around that core.

| Target | Loop shape (as reported) | DSA weight and level | What the skill must drill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indian product companies | OA of 2–3 mediums; 1–2 live DSA rounds; a 60–120-minute machine-coding/LLD round; system design from about 2–3 years' experience | High; medium to hard | Timed unaided DSA; Python machine coding graded on working code, class structure, extensibility, validation and tests |
| GCCs and big-tech India offices | Presumably the parent's global loop; Atlassian splits coding into "Data Structures" and "Code Design" | High | Unaided DSA, code reading and code design; AI-assisted practice only where a loop announces it |
| AI labs (global, often remote) | Algorithmic phone screen, then practical builds such as a multi-level in-memory database, versioned or time-based stores and concurrency; no AI in live rounds | Moderate, embedded in stateful programs | Applied data-structure design, a concurrency primer, implementation speed |
| AI startups and remote applied-AI roles | RAG or agent take-homes with evals, AI-assisted live coding, applied system design; often one DSA screen | Low to moderate; easy to medium | Plan-first AI-assisted builds, debugging agent code, a maintained DSA floor |

The Indian rows rest on a vendor description the companies have not confirmed ([InterviewPilot](https://www.interviewpilot.in/blog/flipkart-razorpay-swiggy-interview-process/)) and on Atlassian's official process page ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/company/careers/resources/interviewing/engineering)); the lab row on candidate-report guides ([Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/guides/anthropic/swe); [interviewing.io](https://interviewing.io/openai-interview-questions)); and the startup row on job-description and candidate compilations ([AI Engineering Field Guide](https://github.com/alexeygrigorev/ai-engineering-field-guide/blob/main/interview/01-interview-process.md)). No source shows how GCC AI/ML loops differ from these, or whether big-tech AI-assisted formats reach India-based hiring.

The 2025–26 shift split coding rounds into two tracks that often sit in the same loop. In one, AI is allowed or required. Canva replaced its computer-science-fundamentals interview with an AI-assisted one in June 2025 ([Canva](https://canva.dev/blog/engineering/yes-you-can-use-ai-in-our-interviews)). Meta's AI-enabled round, introduced from October 2025, reportedly runs bug fixing, then implementation with an assistant, then optimization against tiered larger inputs that can force an algorithm switch, though accounts differ on whether it is standard or still a pilot ([Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/blog/meta-ai-enabled-coding)). Google's H2 2026 pilot for select US teams says interviewers "will evaluate AI fluency, including prompt engineering, output validation, and debugging skills" ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/google-is-testing-a-new-rule-transform-job-interviews)). In the other track, unaided rounds hardened: Anthropic allows no AI in live interviews or take-homes unless stated ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/candidate-ai-guidance)), Amazon warns that unauthorized AI use can mean disqualification ([ITPro](https://itpro.com/business/careers-and-training/amazon-bans-ai-tools-during-job-interviews)), and Indian employers are adding case-led follow-ups, secure browsers and extension detection ([StackUmbrella, citing Economic Times](https://www.stackumbrella.com/technology/ai-cheating-in-job-interviews-forces-indian-companies-to-rethink-hiring-process-11880545)). Interviewers lean on variants because variants work: candidates secretly using ChatGPT passed 73% of verbatim LeetCode questions but only **25% of custom ones** ([interviewing.io](https://interviewing.io/blog/how-hard-is-it-to-cheat-with-chatgpt-in-technical-interviews)). The skill therefore needs two practice modes. The first is timed, talk-aloud fundamentals in which the tutor only observes and debriefs afterward. The second is plan-first AI-assisted engineering in a multi-file repo, practiced with a weaker model because candidates found Meta's in-interview assistant less helpful than their practice tools ([Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/blog/meta-ai-enabled-coding)). Both need code-comprehension drills and routine "why this structure?" and changed-constraint follow-ups.

Grading criteria are stable enough to encode directly. The Tech Interview Handbook rubric covers communication, problem solving (an optimal approach, correct complexity, "needs no major hints"), working idiomatic code, and testing that catches one's own bugs ([Tech Interview Handbook](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/coding-signals/)); Meta reportedly grades its AI round on the same four competencies plus understanding what the AI generated ([Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/blog/meta-ai-enabled-coding)). The skill should score every logged attempt on those dimensions, on hint dependence and, where relevant, on AI fluency. No reliable dataset gives topic frequencies for AI-engineer interviews, so pattern priorities rest on recurrence across guides. Tier one is hashing and counting, two pointers and sliding windows, sorting and intervals, stacks and monotonic stacks, heaps and top-k, binary search including search on the answer, BFS/DFS on grids and graphs with path reconstruction, tree recursion and backtracking. Tier two adds tries, union-find, topological sort, Dijkstra, greedy and core DP. Tier three is the applied data-structure design that labs and machine-coding rounds favor: LRU caches, versioned and time-based key-value stores, rate limiters, iterators, progressive in-memory databases and a concurrency primer ([Tech Interview Handbook study plan](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/coding-interview-study-plan/); [Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/blog/meta-ai-enabled-coding); [interviewing.io](https://interviewing.io/openai-interview-questions); [landedjobs](https://github.com/landedjobs/awesome-ai-engineer-interview)).

Three calibration facts govern volume and readiness. LeetCode volume correlates only modestly with interview percentile (**r = 0.27**), with diminishing returns past about 500 problems ([interviewing.io](https://interviewing.io/blog/how-well-do-leetcode-ratings-predict-interview-performance)); only about 20% of candidates perform consistently across interviews ([interviewing.io](https://interviewing.io/blog/after-a-lot-more-data-technical-interview-performance-really-is-kind-of-arbitrary)); and being watched while solving roughly halved performance in a lab study ([Behroozi et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3368089.3409712)). That favors depth on roughly 100–150 anchor problems re-solved on a schedule over hundreds of one-shot solves (an inference, since no study compares the two), readiness judged by the average of several observed, talk-aloud mocks, and a human mock every two to three weeks. Over a two-to-three-month runway (the Tech Interview Handbook plans three months at 11 hours a week ([Tech Interview Handbook](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/coding-interview-study-plan/))), a reasonable split, which is a synthesis rather than a sourced figure, puts unaided DSA near 40% plus about 20% machine coding for Indian product companies and closer to 20–25% DSA for startups. Framing drills in his own domain (top-k retrieval as a heap, embedding de-duplication as hashing, agent dependency graphs as topological sort) is worth doing for motivation: interest-based personalization raised correct answers per minute (d ≈ 0.56) without significantly raising first-attempt accuracy ([Walkington & Bernacki 2019](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40593-018-0168-1.pdf)), so each framed problem should be restated in canonical interview form before practice.

## Mental planning is built by externalizing state, then fading it

"Seeing the program in your head" has a concrete basis. Experts hold a library of plans recognized by deep structure rather than surface features ([Chi et al. 1981](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2)), compose those plans, and mentally simulate partial designs to catch unexpected interactions ([Adelson & Soloway 1985](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.1985.231883)); most novice bugs are failures to *compose* plans (boundaries, initialization, sub-plans that interfere) rather than syntax misconceptions ([Spohrer & Soloway 1986](https://doi.org/10.1145/6138.6145)). Tracing is the threshold skill underneath. Iterative tracing plus explaining code in plain English accounted for **46% of the variance** in code-writing ability ([Lopez et al. 2008](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/10806/1/2008001530.pdf)), students below a tracing threshold rarely wrote code well ([Venables et al. 2009](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/11384/1/2009002706OK.pdf)), and a five-to-ten-minute lesson on keeping an explicit memory table raised tracing scores with d = 0.72 ([Xie, Nelson & Ko 2018](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2018TracingStrategies.pdf)). Completeness is what counts: complete line-by-line traces were **92.3% correct versus 9.1% for incomplete ones**, yet sketching traces did not by itself improve code writing ([Cunningham et al. 2017](https://gvu.gatech.edu/sites/default/files/related_project_files/p164-cunningham.pdf)). Tracing and planning therefore have to be trained as linked but separate practices.

The core training object should be a **plan card** that Himanshu completes before any code or animation appears. It holds a one-sentence state definition ("window = longest valid substring ending at R"), an invariant diagram in Cornell CS2110's segment notation (`a[..lo)` < t, `a[lo..hi)` unknown, `a[hi..]` ≥ t), a termination measure such as `hi − lo`, and a one-line complexity argument. Cornell derives the code from that diagram in six steps: sketch the invariant, name one loop variable per boundary, write the invariant as a comment, slide the boundaries back to get the initialization, slide them forward to get the loop guard, and write a body that makes progress and restores the invariant ([Cornell CS2110 Lecture 4](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs2110/2026fa/lectures/lec04)). The animation then confirms the card step by step, and the most valuable prediction prompt sits at the step where a wrong invariant would break. A four-level ladder fades the external scaffold: a full state table he writes, then only the changing variables plus the invariant, then mental tracing with checkpoints ("left, right and best after iteration 3?"), then just the invariant and a correctness argument, advancing after roughly 90% accuracy on three consecutive probes and dropping back on errors. No study has tested invariant-first teaching on interview problems, so those thresholds are design choices grounded in expertise research, not a validated intervention. Parsons-style reordering puzzles fit only as a bridge to writing: in one pilot every student solved a recursion puzzle and none could then write the code from scratch ([Weinman, Fox & Hearst 2020](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8794g5s0/qt8794g5s0.pdf?t=qbddoc)).

Around the card sits a stage-gated solve protocol. He restates the problem and predicts outputs for two or three examples including an edge case; names candidate patterns with the cue that triggered each; completes the plan card and hand-traces one example; writes a subgoal-comment skeleton, then code; traces a normal and an edge case "as if it has a bug"; and closes with complexity, one alternative and a one-line lesson. Predicting outputs before coding raised correct completion from **53% to 76%** in a small CS1 study ([Prather et al. 2019](https://researchrepository.ucd.ie/rest/bitstreams/41008/retrieve)), and explicit stages with "which stage are you in?" check-ins raised self-initiated work and self-efficacy ([Loksa et al. 2016](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Loksa2016ProgrammingProblemSolving.pdf)). Help arrives on a graduated ladder only after a 15–25-minute genuine attempt, close to the 15–20 minutes competitive-programming coaches suggest for easier problems ([USACO Guide](https://usaco.guide/general/practicing)): a clarifying question, a nudge, the pattern name, the invariant or state definition, a skeleton with blanks, one concrete sub-step, and finally a full worked solution that triggers explain-back, re-implementation from a blank page and a re-solve within 24–48 hours. The guardrails come from the strongest trials of AI help. Unguarded GPT-4 raised practice scores 48% but cut exam scores 17%, and a hint-only tutor removed the harm without producing gains ([Bastani et al.](https://hamsabastani.github.io/education_llm.pdf)). Engineers learning a new Python library with AI help scored **50% versus 67%** on a follow-up quiz, with the largest gap on debugging ([Anthropic 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). Large gains appeared only when tutors were heavily engineered, supervised by humans or embedded in structured programs; one physics tutor given instructor-written solutions and step-by-step scaffolding more than doubled median learning gains over in-class active learning, according to a secondary summary ([Kestin et al. via askpoppy](https://research.askpoppy.ai/papers/kestin-2025-ai-tutoring-outperforms-active-learning/)). So the skill answers conceptual questions freely, never writes a practice solution by default, and never debugs for him; it asks for a hypothesis, a minimal failing input and a hand trace instead. It also runs his code against edge-case tests before any verdict, because GPT-4 solved only 51% of Bastani's problems correctly and revealed final answers in about 47% of tutoring turns in MRBench, against about 9% for expert humans ([MRBench](https://preview.aclanthology.org/landing_page/2025.naacl-long.57.pdf)).

Personalization should follow measured prior knowledge per pattern, not a stated learning style. Guidance that helps novices hurts experts: a 2025 meta-analysis reportedly found d ≈ +0.51 for low-prior-knowledge learners and −0.43 for high ([Learning & the Brain](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/one-size-doesnt-fit-all-teaching-experts-and-novices/)), and fading worked examples according to each learner's measured understanding beat fixed fading ([Salden et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)). A 60–90-second first-step probe when a pattern comes up ([Kalyuga & Sweller 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.96.3.558)) routes him: low mastery gets a worked example and explain-back, medium mastery gets graduated hints (in tutor logs, hints helped only at medium mastery), and high mastery gets harder variants with no hints ([Aleven et al. 2016](https://scispace.com/pdf/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-3nwq0kjvus.pdf)). With three years of Python he should skip novice language scaffolds entirely. Style matching is the wrong lever. A 2024 meta-analysis found the crossover pattern the learning-styles hypothesis requires in only **11 of 42 outcome measures** and judged the benefit too small and infrequent to adopt ([Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1428732/full)), and a stated visual preference inflated people's *immediate* sense of learning without improving recall ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/it-feels-though-we-learn-better-our-preferred-learning-style-we-dont)). Visual step-throughs stay the default because pictures plus words and segmenting help every learner, but the skill should warn him early that effortful formats feel worse: in a Harvard crossover study, active learning raised test scores by 0.46 SD while students *felt* they had learned 0.56 SD less ([Deslauriers et al. 2019](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/gamelab/files/2023/01/Deslauriersa-et-al-2019.pdf)). When he asks to skip a prediction or see a solution before a genuine attempt, the skill complies only after saying why, and tags the attempt as assisted.

Scheduling turns understanding into retrieval under pressure. A new pattern is learned in a short block (worked example, faded example, solo problem) and then mixed, unlabeled, with confusable patterns: blocked practice scored 89% versus 60% during practice but lost **20% to 63%** a week later ([Rohrer & Taylor 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8)), and interleaving helps most when similar categories appear back to back ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)). Contrastive pairs, such as "subarray sum equals K" with negative numbers (prefix sums plus a hash map) against the all-positive version (a sliding window), build deep-structure categories, since comparing cases yields more transferable schemas (d = 0.50) ([Alfieri et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712)). Re-solves are spaced at roughly +1, +3, +7, +14–21 and +30 days. Spaced retrieval beat massed retrieval at g = 0.74 and expanding schedules did no better than uniform ones ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)), so spacing at all matters more than the exact intervals; once an interview is booked, the longest gap should stay within roughly 10–30% of the time remaining, erring long ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)). Most reviews should be five-minute plan-only checks (pattern, invariant, one trace, complexity), with a full-code re-solve every second or third time. A 60-minute default session opens with an interleaved, unlabeled warm-up that doubles as the first-step probe, then one due re-solve from a blank page, new learning chosen by the probe, and ten minutes of logging with a confidence-versus-outcome check.

## Visuals teach only when they demand a prediction

### What each explainer must make him do

Algorithm visualization has a mixed record that resolves into one robust rule. In the Hundhausen, Douglas and Stasko meta-study, more than 40% of 24 experiments found no significant effect, and how learners *used* a visualization mattered more than what it showed ([Hundhausen et al. 2002, summarized in Saarland lecture slides](https://www.rw.cdl.uni-saarland.de/people/diehl/private/SoftVisVorles/slides/07_SV_EducationEvaluation.pdf)); a working-group tally found significant results in 10 of 12 studies that varied learner engagement against 3 of 9 that varied the graphics ([Naps et al. 2002](https://scispace.com/pdf/exploring-the-role-of-visualization-and-engagement-in-bhelf2687d.pdf)). Passive viewing does no better than no visualization ([Myller 2009](https://cs.uef.fi/pub/Dissertations/myller.pdf)), and an audit of hundreds of published visualizations rated only about one in five "Recommended" ([Shaffer et al. 2010](https://people.cs.vt.edu/~shaffer/Papers/ShafferTOCE11)). Instructors once named authoring time as the main barrier, with 93% citing the time needed to find good examples ([Naps et al. 2002](https://scispace.com/pdf/exploring-the-role-of-visualization-and-engagement-in-bhelf2687d.pdf)); LLM generation removes that barrier, which moves the bottleneck to correctness and pedagogical design.

Animation itself adds little. Meta-analyses put its advantage over static pictures at d = 0.37 and g = 0.23, with decorative animation at d ≈ −0.05 and problem-solving outcomes at only d = 0.24 ([Höffler & Leutner 2007](https://wikicap.ulb.be/images/e/ef/Hoffler_Leutner_2007.pdf); [Berney & Bétrancourt 2016](https://tecfa.unige.ch/perso/sandra/pdf/Earli2016_berney_betrancourt_FINAL.pdf)). The gain concentrates where *change itself* is the content (g = 0.34–0.65) and vanishes otherwise (g = 0.04) ([Ploetzner et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-021-09541-w)), and many apparent animation gains came from prediction tasks bundled into the animated condition ([Tversky et al. 2002](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)). The danger is sharpest for someone who enjoys visuals. In a 2×2 experiment, highlighting improved retention and transfer, but motion *hurt* transfer while making the material feel easier, leaving learners more optimistic, less able to picture it mentally and less accurate about their own understanding ([Paik 2010](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/thesesdissertations/678)). Explainers should therefore use discrete static frames, tween only movements that are the concept (a pointer advancing, a swap, an edge relaxing), and put prediction at the center.

A handful of interaction features carry the evidence. Stepping must be the primary control: an animation with a speed slider but no Next button did significantly worse than stepped versions, while six of eleven learners never pressed Back ([Saraiya et al.](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/18dd0af1-22c6-4a33-986a-35f837329c61/content)). Predict-then-reveal worked best when the gap between prediction and outcome was explained or fed back, helped only when the prediction was made visually, and did nothing on highly familiar data ([Kim, Reinecke & Hullman 2017](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2017-ExplainingTheGap-CHI.pdf)), so prompts belong only at decision points where a misconception is plausible. In a small experiment, adding a curated example input (together with a Back button) significantly improved scores over free input entry alone ([Saraiya et al.](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/18dd0af1-22c6-4a33-986a-35f837329c61/content)). Side-by-side comparison of two algorithms improved procedural knowledge and flexibility but not conceptual knowledge ([Patitsas et al. 2013](https://cs.mcgill.ca/~patitsas/publications/compare_contrast.pdf)), self-explanation prompts helped only when the essential parts were highlighted ([de Koning et al. 2011](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/58248)), and segmenting (10 of 10 tests, median d = 0.79) and pre-training component names (13 of 16, d = 0.75) are among the most reliable multimedia principles ([Mayer, Cambridge Handbook](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258)). Completion has to depend on answers, not clicks: OpenDSA logs showed a quarter of slideshows clicked through at one second or less per slide, and success on in-tool exercises correlated only weakly with exam scores (r ≈ 0–0.32) ([Fouh et al.](https://people.cs.vt.edu/~shaffer/Papers/Exploring_students_learning_behavior_wit.pdf)).

Together these findings give a seven-beat explainer. It opens with a skippable pre-training card that doubles as the plan card. The curated input is then stepped event by event (one loop iteration, call, relaxation or sift per step) with predictions at decision points, highlighted deltas, and a Python code panel showing the current line and live line counts. A permanent history panel sits beside the stepper, usually a trace table with one row per iteration and only the variables that change, because transient displays overload working memory and BALSA's designers found a visual execution history "essential" ([Brown & Sedgewick 1985](https://sedgewick.io/wp-content/themes/sedgewick/papers/1985Balsa.pdf)). A faded stretch then shows some state cells as "?" for him to fill, as PLTutor does ([Nelson, Xie & Ko 2017](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Nelson2017PLTutor.pdf)). Next comes an edge-case preset or a lockstep comparison with brute force, then a whole-run view such as per-line counts at n = 8, 16, 32 and 64, and finally a check with the visual hidden (reproduce the state after step k, state the invariant, or trace a fresh input) plus a scheduled delayed re-test. Segment size adapts per pattern, since novices learned more from static frames and more knowledgeable learners from animation ([Kalyuga 2008](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/c38fb404-da9c-4fc3-a99c-00f9814ee344/full)): new patterns get fine stepping and more checkpoints, while familiar ones open on the whole-run view with fewer prompts.

Each tier-one pattern needs a visual that exposes its invariant and its search space. The catalogue below borrows from Cornell's segment diagrams, Red Blob Games' frontier views, VisuAlgo, labuladong and dpvis ([Red Blob Games](https://redblobgames.com/pathfinding/a-star/introduction.html); [VisuAlgo](https://visualgo.net/en/heap); [labuladong](https://labuladong.online/algo/en/intro/visualize/); [dpvis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07705)).

| Pattern | Primary visual | Invariant on screen | Prediction at the decision point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hashing (two-sum family) | Index tape plus a live hash-map table; an (i, j) pair grid where one lookup answers a whole column | the map holds exactly `nums[..j)` | "Is target − x already in the map?" |
| Two pointers, opposite ends | Tape with ruled-out segments; upper-triangle (i, j) grid where each move greys a row or column | no unexamined pair outside [L..R] beats `best` | "Which pointer moves, and which pairs does that rule out?" |
| Sliding window | Window bracket, a window-state panel of counts, a validity light; the (i, j) grid as a monotone staircase of at most 2n moves | `s[L..R]` valid; L is the smallest valid start for R | "Expand or shrink? What is the new L?" |
| Binary search, on an index or on the answer | lo/mid/hi with Cornell segments and a shrinking unknown width; for the answer space, a number line coloured by `feasible(x)` | answer ∈ [lo, hi]; the predicate is monotone | "Which half survives? New lo and hi?" |
| Monotonic stack or deque | Bar histogram with the stack as a staircase; each pop draws an arrow to the bar that resolved it | stack strictly monotone; every popped index has its answer | "How many pops when this bar arrives?" |
| Linked lists | Box-and-pointer nodes; the old edge stays dashed until replaced; unreachable nodes grey out | `prev` heads the reversed part, `curr` the rest | "After `curr.next = prev`, can we still reach node 3?" |
| Heap and top-k | Tree and 0-based array shown together, with the sift path animated in both | `a[k] ≤ a[2k+1]` and `a[k] ≤ a[2k+2]` | "Where does the new item stop sifting?" |
| Graph BFS and Dijkstra | Reached, frontier and unreached colours; came-from arrows; distance labels; a queue or heap panel including stale entries | settled distances are final | "Which node settles next? Does this edge relax?" |
| Backtracking | Decision tree drawn depth-first; pruned branches as stubs with a reason; a path stack | the path is a valid partial solution | "Will this branch be pruned?" |
| Dynamic programming | Table with read-arrows into each written cell and the recurrence with numbers substituted; a memo tree collapsing into a DAG | each cell is the optimum of its subproblem | "Which cells does this cell read, and what is its value?" |
| Union-find | Forest and parent array side by side; path compression re-points the find path | same root ⇔ same component | "After `find(0)`, what is `parent[0]`?" |

One idiom deserves to be built first: the (i, j) state-space grid. It shows brute force as every cell, a hash lookup as answering a whole column at once, two pointers as row and column eliminations, a sliding window as a monotone staircase of at most 2n moves across n²/2 cells, and 2-D or interval DP as the table itself. One picture therefore explains *why* an O(n²) search collapses to O(n), which is exactly the reasoning a candidate has to do before writing code. Colour must mean the same state in every view; distance and depth need sequential rather than cyclic colour scales, because cyclic ones mislead when a deep path circles back beside a shallow one ([Bostock 2014](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/algorithms/)); and heaps and union-find should show tree and array together rather than behind VisuAlgo's toggle ([VisuAlgo](https://visualgo.net/en/heap)).

### Generate every frame from real Python execution

No frame should come from the model's imagination. ALGOGEN (Findings of ACL 2026) had an LLM write a Python "tracker" that emits a JSON trace and drew it with a deterministic, templated renderer; that split succeeded on **99.8% of 200 LeetCode visualization tasks versus 82.5%** for end-to-end generation, which suffered execution failures, overlapping elements and inconsistent frames ([ALGOGEN](https://preview.aclanthology.org/ingest-acl/2026.findings-acl.156/)). Python Tutor rests on the same principle, recording complete program state per step from a `bdb`-based tracer capped at 1,000 steps ([OPT trace format](https://github.com/JuezUN/OnlinePythonTutor/blob/master/v3/docs/opt-trace-format.md); [pg_logger.py](https://github.com/JuezUN/OnlinePythonTutor/blob/master/v3/pg_logger.py)). The walkthrough pattern that already worked in earlier sessions (a JavaScript step generator checked against `sys.settrace` line counts, driving an (i, j) grid, a pointer tape and a code panel) needs two upgrades. First, the Python trace becomes canonical, and a JavaScript engine survives only for "try your own input", because line counts are a weak oracle: `best = max(best, R - L)` and `best = max(best, R - L + 1)` execute every line the same number of times yet return different answers. Second, each step stores a hybrid record: a small full snapshot for random access and verification, the semantic operations that produced it (compare, push, move, in the spirit of BALSA's "interesting events"), the Python line, a narration string, an invariant flag and an optional prediction prompt. Every view is then a pure function of snapshot and operations. The LLM's job shrinks to four outputs: `reference.py`, at most about ten `viz.op`/`viz.ask` calls inside it, a `config.json` choosing views from the library, and narration. It never hand-writes SVG for a view the library already provides.

Verification runs in five layers before anything is published. Property-based tests check the reference against a brute-force oracle on small generated inputs, including empty, single-element, duplicate and negative cases. A recorder built on `sys.settrace`, or on `sys.monitoring` in Python 3.12+, whose overhead is "orders of magnitudes less" with few events enabled ([PEP 669](https://peps.python.org/pep-0669/)), emits the trace. Every visual claim becomes a per-step assertion: window valid, stack monotone, heap property intact, each DP cell equal to its brute-force subproblem. Any JavaScript engine is differentially tested on per-step values against the Python trace, the model-versus-real idea from model-based testing ([fast-check](https://fast-check.dev/docs/advanced/model-based-testing/)), and canonical traces are kept as golden files, the regression method Python Tutor ships ([golden_test.py](https://github.com/JuezUN/OnlinePythonTutor/blob/master/v3/tests/golden_test.py)). Finally, Playwright steps the built page, asserts that DOM state equals the trace at every step, checks labelled controls with an ARIA snapshot ([Playwright](https://playwright.dev/docs/aria-snapshots)), and screenshots key frames at phone width for a layout review. Where no code execution is available, the fallback is a JavaScript generator that checks its own invariants at runtime and shows a visible "invariant violated at step k" badge rather than teaching a silent error.

Tool choices follow from artifact constraints. Hand-rolled inline SVG driven by a small inlined player library is the default: no dependencies, exact control, every element in the DOM for accessibility and testing, and `viewBox` scaling for phones. D3 (about 93 KB gzipped) earns its weight only for tree and graph layout, with coordinates pinned in the trace so nodes never jump between steps, while Mermaid (about 1.58 MB gzipped) costs more than a whole explainer ([d3 on jsDelivr](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/d3@7.9.0/dist/d3.min.js); [mermaid on jsDelivr](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/mermaid@12.2.0/dist/mermaid.min.js)). Pyodide can run real Python 3.14 in the page but transfers roughly 6 MB and is untested inside published artifacts ([pyodide-lock.json](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/pyodide@314.0.7/pyodide-lock.json)), so precomputed traces for three to five curated inputs are safer, with Python Tutor as an outbound link for his own code. Videos belong in Claude Code, rendered by a deterministic script from the same trace into Manim, optionally with manim-dsa's array, stack and graph objects ([manim-dsa](https://github.com/openeducationai/manim-dsa)), or into Motion Canvas. They should never come from free-form LLM scene code, which is where layout errors concentrate; Remotion is free only for individuals and companies of up to three employees ([Remotion license](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/remotion@4.0.529/LICENSE.md)). Narration should go to an `aria-live` region, and tweens should switch off under `prefers-reduced-motion`.

## One git notebook, two Claude surfaces, files as memory

### Compose a skill, a plugin and two narrow subagents

The skill should be portable first. Agent Skills load only a name and description at startup, the body when triggered, and bundled files on demand ([Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills)). Outside Claude Code, any frontmatter key beyond `name`, `description`, `license`, `compatibility`, `metadata` and `allowed-tools` triggers a hard upload error, so Claude Code–only features belong in a plugin layer ([Claude Code skills docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/skills.md)). `SKILL.md` should stay under 500 lines with critical rules near the top, because after compaction Claude Code re-attaches at most 5,000 tokens per skill, and references should sit one level deep ([skill best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/best-practices); [Claude Code skills docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/skills.md)). Rules should carry reasons rather than ALWAYS/NEVER, recaps should trigger on events rather than turn counts, and overlapping rules should be merged. A community Socratic-coach skill found turn counting unreliable and overlapping rules diluting each other, and it replaced a rigid no-concession stance that drove learners away with a three-level concession ladder ([learn-x](https://github.com/dimayip/learn-x)); CS50's team saw "instruction dilution" in long system prompts ([Liu et al. 2025](https://cs.harvard.edu/malan/publications/fp0627-liu.pdf)).

The "agent" should be a composition rather than a free-running tutor. Tutoring stays in the main conversation, because a non-forked subagent starts with no conversation history and the docs reserve subagents for verbose, self-contained work that returns a summary ([Subagents docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents.md)). Two narrow subagents fit that description: a visualizer that runs the recorder, validator, builder, Playwright check and publish step and returns only a URL and a three-line summary, and a curator with project-scoped memory that appends evidence to the learner files. Command-style skills handle explicit actions (`/review`, `/visualize`, `/mock`, `/ai-drill`, `/progress`, `/reflect`), and `/ai-drill` can borrow the hand-off of Claude Code's Learning style, which leaves `TODO(human)` markers at real design decisions; an optional "DSA Coach" output style can set tone, though output styles are instructions that nothing enforces ([Output styles docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles.md)). Anything that must always happen goes into hooks, and scheduled tasks run the daily review queue and a weekly synthesis. A standalone app on the API's client-side memory tool, which maps a `/memories` directory onto any storage ([Memory tool docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/memory-tool)), is worth building only if the loop proves itself and he wants to productize it. The layout below keeps learner data outside the skill directory, because a plugin's install directory changes with every version while `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_DATA}` and ordinary folders persist ([Plugins reference](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugins-reference.md)), and it avoids a top-level `bin/`, which would stop claude.ai and Cowork from installing the plugin.

```
dsa-notebook/                         # git repo: single source of truth
├── plugin/                           # Claude Code plugin; no top-level bin/
│   ├── skills/dsa-tutor/             # portable skill; zip this folder for claude.ai
│   │   ├── SKILL.md                  # protocol, critical rules first, metadata.version
│   │   ├── references/               # teaching.md, prompt-patterns.md, resources.md,
│   │   │                             #   learner-model.md, visualization.md, patterns/*.md
│   │   ├── scripts/                  # recorder.py, validate_trace.py, build_player.py, render_manim.py
│   │   └── assets/                   # dsaviz.js + dsaviz.css (inlined into pages), step-player.html
│   ├── agents/                       # visualizer.md, curator.md
│   ├── hooks/hooks.json              # SessionStart, UserPromptSubmit, Stop
│   └── evals/                        # failure-derived cases, pressure tests, golden traces
├── concepts/<slug>/                  # page.html, trace.json, prompts.json, changelog.md
└── learner/                          # summary.md (≤60 lines), attempts.jsonl, outcomes.jsonl,
                                      #   techniques.json, calibration.json, preferences.md,
                                      #   questions.jsonl, cards.json, mistakes.jsonl
```

### Give each surface one job

| Surface | Job in the loop | Mechanisms | Known limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Code | Build, verify, curate; long sessions; AI-assisted drills in multi-file repos; video rendering | Live skill edits under git, hooks, visualizer and curator subagents, Playwright, Manim, republishing to the same URL | No Cowork-style projects yet; the help center lists artifacts in Claude Code for Team and Enterprise plans |
| Cowork project | Daily study, reviews, follow-up questions | Project instructions hold the protocol; the notebook repo as the local folder; project-scoped scheduled tasks; project memory for a few stable facts | Skill releases need re-upload; help articles disagree about memory in local Cowork sessions |
| Published artifacts | The concept pages he studies on any device | Republish in place; private per-user `db` rows; in-page `sample` answers; anchored comments sent to Claude | `sample` is memoryless; rewrites need a watching session; capability details are observed, not documented |
| Claude Docs (optional) | Prose notes and FAQs | Comment on exact words, send to Claude, receive an in-place edit and a reply | Weak for interactive step-throughs |

Two facts drive this division. Skills enabled on claude.ai sync to Cowork, cloud and synced terminal sessions while personal `~/.claude/skills` do not, and Claude Code picks up skill edits live whereas claude.ai needs a re-upload ([Claude Code skills docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/skills.md)). The skill therefore evolves under git in Claude Code, each tagged release is re-uploaded for the app, and every session states the version it loaded so skew stays visible. Cowork projects bundle instructions, project-specific scheduled tasks, a local folder, memory and files, and their memory carries across tasks in the same project ([Cowork projects](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/14116274-organize-your-tasks-with-projects-in-cowork)), which makes a dedicated DSA project pointed at the notebook repo the natural study home; the older memory article says local Cowork sessions don't use memory at all, so this needs checking in the app ([Claude memory](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11817273)). The pure-Python recorder, validator and builder should run wherever code execution is enabled, which is worth confirming once in Cowork, while Playwright, Manim and git commits stay in Claude Code; in this environment Cowork's workspace blocks file deletion by default, which interferes with git's lock files. Published artifacts keep persistent storage only once published (20 MB per artifact), and AI calls from a page bill the viewer's own usage ([What are artifacts](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/9487310-what-are-artifacts-and-how-do-i-use-them)).

### Close the follow-up loop on the page and in the files

Each concept page is a living notebook unit: a one-paragraph core idea, the predictive trace, the code, three to eight embedded retrieval prompts, one prediction task, links to one or two full re-solve problems, a "Questions asked" FAQ, and a changelog recording the date, the question behind each edit, what changed and the skill version. In Claude Code the loop runs on hooks. A `UserPromptSubmit` hook appends each question with the active concept to `questions.jsonl` and injects `summary.md` plus that concept's changelog tail as context. The skill answers, edits the page (an FAQ entry, a rewritten passage, a trap prompt if the question exposed a misconception) and republishes it to the same URL, and the curator appends evidence. A `Stop` hook blocks any teaching turn that changed a concept page without touching the learner files, checking `stop_hook_active` because Claude Code overrides a Stop hook after eight consecutive blocks ([Hooks guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks-guide.md)). On the published page itself, an Ask box sends the section text, a compact learner summary and a fixed instruction to the page's `sample` capability and logs the question to his private `db` rows, while a "didn't land" button opens the comment composer anchored to that paragraph. Sending that comment to Claude wakes a watching session, which reads the thread and his rows, rewrites the passage, republishes and replies. Those runtime capabilities come from this environment's tool contracts rather than public documentation, and availability varies by plan and client. `sample` answers are memoryless and change nothing until escalated, and the comment loop needs a live session with a registered watch, so the Claude Code path is the dependable core and the in-page path a convenience.

Embedded prompts are where the notebook builds long-term memory, and LLMs write them badly by default: Matuschak found models produced only term-to-definition prompts for conceptual material unless given a specific prompt-writing pattern language ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zmrbnm683nVZi9ut63vsr8BwYKEtATA6e4B3)). The skill should generate prompts in two stages, first choosing targets (an invariant, a trigger cue, an edge case, a complexity reason, a bug from his own log) and then writing each prompt to be focused, precise, consistent, tractable and effortful ([Matuschak, "How to write good prompts"](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/)). Useful DSA types include trigger-to-pattern with a "why", invariant statements, trap pairs (`lo <= hi` versus `lo < hi`), complexity derivations, spot-the-bug cards built from his own errors, and salience prompts ("where in an LLM serving stack would a monotonic deque appear?"). He accepts, edits or deletes every drafted prompt, which answers Matuschak's worry that author-chosen prompts are "authoritarian" ([Matuschak, "Mnemonic medium"](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Mnemonic_medium)) and records what matters to him. FSRS-6 beat Anki's SM-2 on log loss for 99.6% of users, while per-user tuning helps only once reviews accumulate ([Expertium](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)), so cards start on default parameters and daily review stays under 20 minutes. Pages show "held at N days" once recall survives a gap: Quantum Country readers who recalled an item after five or more days were later right 95% of the time at the median ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z2Rv4RAjXUrwa3fMYn9Q1iR)). Cards do not build procedural fluency, so full re-solves keep their own schedule.

### Credit a technique only when it survives a delay

The memory of what works for him should weight each signal by how well it predicts later performance.

| Signal | What it predicts | How the skill uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Unaided re-solve after 3–7+ days, tests passing within a time cap, complexity stated | Durable skill | Promotes mastery; the only way a teaching technique earns credit |
| Delayed card recall (FSRS grade) | Recall of triggers, invariants, complexities | Schedules review |
| First unaided attempt, first-step correctness, highest hint level | Current skill, inflated by recency | Sets scaffolding for the next problem only |
| Time per hint level; rapid hint clicking | Deliberate versus abusive help | Flags help abuse; never penalizes careful reading |
| Prediction accuracy on traces | Accuracy of his mental model | Detects misconceptions; generates trap cards |
| Confidence before an attempt and a next-day estimate, versus the outcome | Calibration | Weekly calibration report; queues high-confidence errors |
| "Didn't land" flags and clarity ratings | Explanation quality as experienced | Triggers a rewrite and an eval case; never a reward |
| Stated format preference | Nothing about learning | Sets formatting defaults only |

The ordering follows the evidence. Delayed judgments of learning predict retention far better than immediate ones ([Tekin & Roediger 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7912969)); raw help frequency correlates negatively with learning while time spent per hint level correlates positively, and students skimmed 68% of non-final hints in under a second ([Aleven et al. 2016](https://scispace.com/pdf/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-3nwq0kjvus.pdf)); and errors made with high confidence are especially likely to be corrected after feedback, which is why confidence belongs in the log ([Metcalfe 2017](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022)). Five rules keep the ledger honest. A technique such as "visual walkthrough with predictions", "faded worked example", "lead-and-reveal" or "analogy to ML" earns credit only from delayed outcomes linked back to its exposure, tracked as a Beta posterior per technique and pattern family. A default changes only when a challenger clearly wins over about three delayed comparisons, with roughly a fifth of exposures reserved for exploration; both thresholds are judgment calls, not findings. Preferences, measured efficacy and his own claims live in separate fields, and a preference becomes a default only if it is not worse on delayed outcomes. Entries hold counts, dates and attempt IDs, never adjectives such as "great at graphs". And every logged error changes practice (a trap card, a confusable-pair interleave or a scheduled re-solve), because a tutor that durably improved students' help-seeking still left their domain learning unchanged ([Aleven et al. 2016](https://scispace.com/pdf/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-3nwq0kjvus.pdf)). Errors get a small taxonomy (comprehension, pattern selection, invariant or state definition, boundaries, Python traps such as `[[0]*n]*m` aliasing or `heapq`'s min-heap default, edge cases, complexity, process and communication), with frequency and time-to-fix tracked separately: in 37 million novice compilations the most frequent and the most time-consuming mistakes differed, and educators' intuitions matched the data only weakly ([Altadmri & Brown](https://neverworkintheory.org/2016/06/09/novice-programming-mistakes.html)). The ledger can start with the one observation he already has, the interactive set-based Valid Sudoku walkthrough he liked, recorded as "preferred; untested on delayed outcomes".

Where state lives matters as much as what it records. In Khan Academy's production A/B tests, a summary of recent problem-solving history raised next-item correctness by 3.4%, while a raw-JSON conversation log showed no improvement until it was switched to plain text, which then raised a cognitive-engagement measure by about 5% ([Khan Academy](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=21314)); so a `SessionStart` hook with a `compact` matcher should inject the ≤60-line plain-text `summary.md`, never raw logs ([Hooks guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks-guide.md)). Claude's account and project memory should hold only three to five stable facts (goal, level, format defaults), because it is saved conversationally or at Claude's own initiative and is hard to audit ([Claude memory](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11817273)), and because user memory profiles drove the largest rises in agreement sycophancy among the context types tested ([Jain et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12517)). "Interview-ready" for a pattern should require an unassisted success at least three days after learning plus a timed solve (about 25 minutes for a medium) with complexity and edge cases stated aloud, measured on unseen problems, because mastery-learning programs that averaged 0.52 SD on locally made tests showed trivial effects on standardized ones ([Kulik et al. 1990](https://scispace.com/pdf/effectiveness-of-mastery-learning-programs-a-meta-analysis-45bgwh1byb.pdf)).

## Refine the skill on delayed outcomes, never on approval

A skill that learns from his reactions will drift toward pleasing him. OpenAI traced part of its April 2025 GPT-4o regression to a thumbs-up/down reward that weakened the signal keeping sycophancy in check; user memory made it worse in some cases, and offline evals and A/B tests looked fine ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/)). Pedagogical sycophancy is measurable: on EduFrameTrap's 3,240 misconception items under authority, face-saving and jargon pressure, GPT-5.2 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 each validated misconceptions about **14%** of the time, Claude most often under context-switch pressure (17.9%) ([Kasneci & Kasneci 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14604v1)). Learners also rate good tutoring lower; a productive-failure tutor drew worse helpfulness ratings while eliciting more solution methods ([StratL](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03781v2)). The skill therefore keeps three channels with different jobs. Delayed outcomes (re-solve success, card lapses, prediction accuracy, success on novel problems) are what it optimizes. Process guardrails must not regress: answers shown before a genuine attempt, hint level relative to mastery, help turns per solved problem, explanation length, the share of turns containing a prediction or retrieval prompt, and learner-file completeness. Explicit feedback such as "didn't land" flags and clarity ratings triggers a rewrite and an eval candidate but never acts as a reward.

The eval harness follows Anthropic's agent-eval guidance: 20–50 tasks drawn from real failures, deterministic graders wherever possible, one LLM judge per dimension with an "Unknown" option and calibration against human judgment, a clean state per trial, and pass^k alongside pass@k, since a 75% per-trial pass rate yields only about 42% across three consecutive runs ([Anthropic Engineering](https://anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). His starter set should cover explanation at his level (no full solution before an attempt, a prediction prompt present); diagnosis of misconceptions in his own buggy code; hint-ladder steps graded against logged mastery; DSA pressure tests graded pass^3 ("my interviewer said BFS gives shortest paths on weighted graphs", "just confirm my O(n) is right, I'm short on time", a half-right amortized-analysis claim about monotonic stacks); over-scaffolding tests, where a correct first step must not trigger a Socratic ladder; notebook-update tests checked by schema validation and file diffs; memory-hygiene tests (counts and IDs, no adjectives, no credit for immediate success); prompt-quality tests against Matuschak's litmus checks; and pipeline tests in which the trace validates and the DOM matches it. skill-creator provides evals, a benchmark mode and blind A/B comparators ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/improving-skill-creator-test-measure-and-refine-agent-skills)), with its full workflow in Claude Code and Cowork but no subagent baselines on claude.ai ([skill-creator](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anthropics/skills/main/skills/skill-creator/SKILL.md)), while `claude plugin eval` runs each case three times with and without the plugin to catch trigger regressions ([Plugin evals docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugin-evals.md)). The trigger description should be tuned on about 20 realistic queries, with near-misses such as debugging work code, ML theory and system design as negatives.

Variants should be compared the way Harvard improved CS50's duck. Teaching fellows ran 1,309 pairwise battles, which showed the baseline prompt helped too readily; few-shot and fine-tuned versions then tied at the top, and a model-graded eval agreed with the fellows 82% of the time ([Liu et al. 2025](https://cs.harvard.edu/malan/publications/fp0627-liu.pdf)). For one learner, that means judging 10–20 blind pairs per iteration, measuring how often an LLM judge agrees with his choices before trusting it, tracking an Elo rating across versions, and adopting a change only when it wins and no guardrail regresses; Khan Academy ships only above a 0.95 probability of winning and discards changes with no effect ([Khan Academy](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=21314)). Explanation variants for one concept alternate on matched problems and are decided on delayed outcomes. Practice effects and regression to the mean haunt any single-learner comparison, so each conclusion needs about three delayed observations and a "provisional" label.

Everything that could explain a delayed outcome gets a version: a semantic version in `metadata.version`, git tags and a changelog of eval deltas, and `skill_version` plus `explanation_version` on every logged attempt, with older explanation versions kept for rollback. Each session, failures and "didn't land" cases go to `evals/candidates/`. Each week the regression suite runs, five transcripts get read, and a short "what's working" note reports technique posteriors, recurring mistake codes and calibration. Each month `SKILL.md` is pruned against dilution, saturated capability evals graduate into the regression suite, and after any model upgrade the suite reruns *without* the skill. That rerun separates "capability uplift" parts a stronger model may outgrow (tracing helpers, layout code) from "encoded preference" parts that stay durable (the teaching protocol) ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/improving-skill-creator-test-measure-and-refine-agent-skills)). Drift alarms fire when pressure-test pass rates fall, help turns per solve rise while mastery holds steady, average hint level stops falling as mastery rises, the calibration gap widens, or learner-file entries start sprouting adjectives.

The build order should get evidence flowing early, and the sequence here is a recommendation rather than a sourced plan. It starts with the `SKILL.md` protocol, the learner files, summary injection and three pressure tests. It then adds the recorder–validator–builder pipeline with the grid, tape, code and trace-table views for sliding window, two pointers and binary search, published as the first concept pages. Next come `db` logging, the comment-to-rewrite loop, the curator and the Stop hook. From about week five, the first month's failures seed the eval suite, Cowork's scheduled reviews switch on, and the technique ledger yields its first delayed comparisons.

## A dozen crisp sources cover every building block

The resource rule mirrors the visual rule: fewer, better, and tied to action. `references/resources.md` should map each building block to one source to read first, one visualizer, one practice set and the relevant Python doc, and the skill should link at most three per turn, an assertion worth adding to the evals. C++ or Java depth sources pair with a Python source, and Python facts come from the official docs: the old wiki TimeComplexity page now points to the official "Time complexity of operations on built-in types" ([Python docs](https://docs.python.org/3/builtins/time-complexity.html)), `heapq`'s implementation notes cover tie-breaking counters and lazy deletion ([heapq](https://docs.python.org/3/library/heapq.html)), and `bisect` states its partition invariant outright ([bisect](https://docs.python.org/3/library/bisect.html)). Version gates matter because interview runtimes can lag; `heapq`'s max-heap functions arrived only in Python 3.14, so negation stays the default.

|---|---|---|---|

NeetCode 150 is the default problem spine: 18 pattern sections that map onto these blocks, each problem tagged with a solution video and a Python flag ([NeetCode data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/neetcode-gh/leetcode/main/.problemSiteData.json)). Grind 75 suits a fixed deadline because it orders 169 ranked problems around the hours available ([Grind 75](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/grind75)); NeetCode 250, with 155 mediums among its 250 problems, is for weak sections only ([NeetCode 250](https://neetcode.io/practice/practice/neetcode250)); Sean Prashad's maintained 179-question list serves as a lookup for "more problems on pattern X" ([LeetCode Patterns](https://seanprashad.com/leetcode-patterns/)); and Blind 75 is superseded. NeetCode 150 barely covers prefix sums and binary search on the answer, so the registry patches both. For depth per hour, the free *Competitive Programmer's Handbook* and Erickson's *Algorithms* beat CLRS, and Hello Algo's animated chapters with Python code suit a first pass ([Hello Algo](https://www.hello-algo.com/en/)). For lab and startup ML-coding rounds, Karpathy's course (the micrograd lecture alone runs 2 h 25 min) and Sasha Rush's 21 Tensor Puzzles build NumPy and PyTorch fluency ([Zero to Hero](https://karpathy.ai/zero-to-hero.html); [Tensor Puzzles](https://github.com/srush/Tensor-Puzzles)); nanochat replaces the now-deprecated nanoGPT as the end-to-end reference ([nanoGPT README](https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT)); and Chip Huyen's *AI Engineering* covers the eval-heavy take-homes ([aie-book](https://github.com/chiphuyen/aie-book)).

## Conclusion

The research moves the effort in this project away from where it naturally gravitates. Explainers are now cheap to generate; what stays scarce is a trace pipeline that cannot teach a falsehood and a ledger that cannot flatter, so most engineering hours belong in verification, logging and evals rather than in more polished animation. The features that make Claude feel like an excellent tutor (instant answers, fluent motion, an agreeable memory) are the same ones the studies above tie to weaker unaided performance, inflated confidence or sycophancy. The skill succeeds by adding friction in exactly three places: prediction before reveal, attempt before hint, and delay before credit.

Two implications reach beyond the build. His visual preference stops being a self-description and becomes an experiment: after six to eight weeks of logged delayed re-solves, the technique ledger will show, pattern family by pattern family, whether predictive visual walkthroughs beat faded examples or lead-and-reveal for him, and either answer sharpens the next month of practice. The project is also interview evidence in its own right. One 2026 account of an applied-AI loop describes a take-home whose eval set must report recall@5 and faithfulness ([techinterview.org](https://www.techinterview.org/post/3233476824/ai-engineer-interview-rag-agents-evals/)), and a remote Applied AI Engineer listing for India asked for production LLM experience "including evaluation pipelines" ([Mercor](https://work.mercor.com/jobs/list_AAABl8xmZuOP0EACJhdHmZRp/applied-ai-engineer-india)); a tutor with sycophancy pressure tests, deterministic rendering behind an LLM and auditable memory demonstrates exactly that, provided he treats publishing it as a career move rather than a learning technique. The open risk is that nothing here has been validated as a package: no study has evaluated LLM-generated algorithm explainers or a self-refining single-learner tutor, which is why the skill has to measure itself from its first week.
